Internship · ADAG Group, Montreal · Jun–Jul 2026

Data-center cooling CFD

Cooling design for a 160 MW AI data-center campus at a Montreal engineering consultancy, reporting to the Director of Technical Excellence.

Status
Completed
Role
CFD & Design Intern
Software
Cadence Reality DC
Dates
Jun–Jul 2026

The problem

GPU racks put out far more heat than the servers most data halls were designed around, so the cooling has to be modeled before anything is built. Full-detail CFD of a whole campus is slow to set up and solve, so the useful question is how much detail each model actually needs.

What I did

  • Learned Cadence Reality DC from scratch, then built and solved full thermal and airflow models of the campus.
  • Ran a three-tier fidelity study on a 600 kW fan wall cooling unit, modeling it at three levels of detail and comparing each with the high-fidelity result.
  • Compared the tiers on rack-inlet temperatures, temperature profiles 1, 5 and 10 ft from the supply face, cold-aisle velocity and external static pressure, with solve time as the cost. Then picked the cheapest model that stayed within ±5%.
  • Modeled a rear-door heat exchanger for GPU racks at about 50 kW per rack.

Trade-offs

Detail against cost. A simpler model is only worth using if it gives the same answer as the detailed one, within a set tolerance.

Heat rejection against pressure drop. A denser rear-door coil removes more heat, but it adds air-side pressure drop that the server fans have to overcome.

Coursework · ME 331 engine lab, team of three · Spring 2026

Engine misfire diagnosis

Duke’s thermodynamics lab runs a small single-cylinder engine so students can compare a measured PV loop with the ideal Otto cycle. Ours came out flat. I worked on the Python processing and the diagnosis that showed why, and our team proposed changes to the rig.

Status
Completed
Course
ME 331, Duke
Team
Three students
My part
Python analysis, misfire tests, intake adapter CAD

The problem

The lab gives you two signals: cylinder pressure and a tachometer pulse. From those you rebuild cylinder volume, plot the PV loop and compare it with an ideal Otto cycle. Our loops had no sharp pressure rise at top dead center, and the area inside them, which is the indicated work, was a fraction of the ideal.

From two signals to a PV loop

  1. Found each combustion peak in the pressure trace with SciPy’s peak finder. The spacing gave 2,786 rpm, within 0.3% of the tachometer’s 2,778 rpm.
  2. Converted time to crank angle, then to piston position with the slider-crank relation, then to cylinder volume using the bore and clearance volume.
  3. Integrated pressure over volume around each cycle and averaged the cycles to get indicated work.

Three tests, one answer

A flat loop can come from bad combustion or from bad measurement, so we checked the combustion three separate ways.

  • Shape against the ideal cycle

    There is no constant-volume pressure spike at top dead center. Pressure climbs slowly and stays high into the expansion stroke, which looks more like a Diesel cycle’s constant-pressure burn than an Otto cycle.

    No spike at TDC

  • Pressure-ratio misfire test

    Following a method from Cesario et al., we compared the pressure 40° after top dead center with the pressure 40° before it. A healthy burn keeps that ratio above 1. This event scored 0.58.

    0 of 12 events passed

  • Polytropic exponents

    On log-log axes, compression and expansion should be straight lines with exponents near 1.3 to 1.4. Compression gave 1.06, close to isothermal, so the charge wasn’t heating as it should. Expansion gave 0.47: pressure fell more slowly than isentropic, as it would if fuel kept burning late into the stroke.

    n = 1.06 and 0.47

Likely causes

  • Too little air. A partly open throttle or a closed choke makes the mixture rich and the burn incomplete. We saw dense exhaust and soot in the intake during testing.
  • An aging sensor. The Optrand pressure sensor is rated for 1 to 3 years, and the rig has been in use for at least 7, so its calibration was in doubt too.

Redesigning the rig

The rig could only produce PV plots, so we proposed four additions:

  • An intake manifold adapter, modeled in CAD, so the lab’s Omega flow meter can measure airflow again.
  • Fuel flow measured over a timed run, in place of a sight-glass reading.
  • A crank encoder, so volume comes from measured crank angle instead of timing between two tape marks.
  • A load cell on the alternator frame, with an adjustable field supply, to measure brake torque.

We also wrote two new experiments for the course. One follows energy from fuel to indicated, brake and electrical power, with an entropy-generation estimate. The other sweeps the air-fuel ratio across throttle positions.

What I’d do differently

  • Set the choke and throttle before logging, and wait until peak pressure stops rising. The handout specified neither, and both of our runs suffered for it.
  • Check that the pressure sensor reads atmospheric with the engine stopped, before every run.
  • Measure crank angle directly rather than inferring it from cycle timing.

Coursework · EGR 101, Duke’s first-year design course · Team of four · 2024–25

Low-cost thermoelectric fridge

A cheap, portable fridge for low-income households in Bangladesh. Our team of four set six requirements, built the fridge from plywood, foam and a thermoelectric cooler, and tested it. I built the box and wired the electronics. It met three of the five requirements we tested.

Status
Completed
Team
Four students
My part
Built the box, wired the electronics
Result
3 of 5 tested targets met

The brief

Refrigeration is out of reach for many low-income households in Bangladesh. We set out to build a fridge that is cheap to make, light enough for two people to carry, and able to keep food cold for a while when the power goes out.

Requirements and results

How it’s built

I built the box and wired the cooling system: the Peltier module, its fans and the power supply.

  • Insulation. A plywood box lined with XPS foam, with the foam seams staggered so air can’t leak straight through.
  • Sealing. Tape along every edge to trap air and cut convection, and a lid of wood and XPS with a window seal around it.
  • Cooling. A thermoelectric (Peltier) module between two heat sinks. The inside sink and fan pull heat out of the box, and the outside sink and fan dump it into the room.

Why it stopped at 50 °F

The fridge fell from about 65 °F to 50.2 °F in two hours, then leveled off about 10 °F short of the target. With the power off it took more than two hours to warm back to room temperature, so the insulation was doing its job. The limit was the cooling module. A Peltier moves less heat as the temperature difference across it grows, and ours ran out of headroom around 50 °F.

It also used 2.7 kWh a day, about 110 W on average, against a 2.2 kWh target.

What we’d change

  • Cool the Peltier’s hot side better. A cooler hot side lets the cold side go colder with the same module.
  • Cascade two modules, so one cools the hot side of the other. That reaches lower temperatures but uses more power, and we had already missed the energy target.
  • Run the three-month durability test, the one requirement we didn’t test.

School project · CREST Gold Award · 2023–24

Optimizing 3D printing for use in space

With a partner, I tested how 3D printer settings change a part’s strength per gram, with tools printed in orbit as the end use. We designed the samples, built our own test rigs and tested 30 print settings. The project earned a CREST Gold Award from the British Science Association.

Status
Completed
Award
CREST Gold
Team
Two people
Time
90+ hours

The problem

Every spare tool sent to space costs launch mass. Printing tools in orbit means carrying only raw material, but only if the printed parts are strong enough. NASA has been 3D printing in orbit since 2014, with parts close in strength to the same parts printed on the ground.

What we did

  • Picked the loads a hand tool sees: torsion (a wrench), bending (a lever) and impact (a hammer).
  • Tested 30 settings across 7 variables, one variable at a time: infill type, infill density, layer height, print temperature, line width, number of walls and orientation. Testing every combination would have meant millions of samples.
  • Printed in PLA+ rather than ABS, which gives off toxic fumes when melted.
  • Designed the samples in CAD, reworked the torsion sample until it broke cleanly in the middle every time, and notched the impact sample so it broke in the same place.
  • Switched from a 3-point to a cantilever bend test, because we couldn’t apply enough force by hand to break samples in 3-point bending.
  • Filmed every test in slow motion and read breaking angles against a protractor, timing fractures to the nearest 0.004 s.
  • Stored samples with silica gel, since damp PLA turns brittle and would have made early prints weaker than late ones.

Findings

  • Layer orientation alone nearly doubled impact energy, from 3.2 J to 6.3 J, when the layers ran perpendicular to the strike.
  • Two walls gave the best strength per gram in torsion and impact. Extra walls added mass faster than strength.
  • Raising print temperature from 190 °C to 220 °C tripled the torsion breaking angle, from 7° to 22°. Impact energy fell as temperature rose, from 6.3 J at 190 °C to 2.0 J at 230 °C, and bending didn’t change.
  • Above about 50% infill, more infill added mass without much gain in impact strength per gram.

Explore the results

Every setting we tested, from our data tables. Pick a setting and a load. A higher breaking angle means a stronger sample, and the best result in each view is shown in orange.

Results explorerOne sample per setting
Setting
Load

Torsion peaked at 220 °C. Impact energy fell as temperature rose, the opposite of our prediction. Bending barely changed.

Torsion and bending were compared by breaking angle rather than force, so they rank settings rather than give absolute strengths.

What I’d do differently

  • Repeat every measurement. Without repeats we couldn’t tell a real trend from an anomaly.
  • Record how each sample failed. A tool that bends before it breaks warns the user; one that snaps doesn’t.
  • Measure force as well as angle, test a third orientation, and test real tool shapes alongside standard samples.

The next step we proposed was slicing software that picks print settings from a part’s expected loads and the material available.
